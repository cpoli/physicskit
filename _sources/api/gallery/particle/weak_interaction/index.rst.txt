    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="In 1947 Lattes, Occhialini and Powell exposed nuclear emulsion plates on mountaintops and found tracks of a new particle, the charged pion, stopping and decaying into a muon. Every one of those muons had the same track length in the emulsion, about 600 µm. That is the signature of a two-body decay, \pi^+\to\mu^++\nu_\mu: in the pion&#x27;s rest frame the muon gets one fixed momentum, however many times the decay is repeated. The muon in turn decayed to an electron with a continuous range of energies, the three-body signature of \mu^+\to e^++\nu_e+\bar\nu_\mu. The whole \pi\to\mu\to e chain was visible in a single photograph.">

    <div class="sphx-glr-thumbcontainer" tooltip="By 1930 beta decay looked like it broke energy conservation. If a nucleus emits only an electron, A\to B + e^-, two-body kinematics gives the electron a single, fixed energy, essentially the whole Q-value. Instead, electrons come out with every energy from zero up to Q. Ellis and Wooster (1927) caught the decays of radium E (210\ Bi, Q=1.16 MeV) in a calorimeter and found the heat per decay matched the spectrum&#x27;s mean, about a third of Q, not its endpoint. The rest of the energy was not being deposited at all.">
